## 2. The core human drives — Josh Kaufman, *The Personal MBA*

All human beings have four Core Human Drives that have a profound influence on our decisions and actions:

**The Drive to Acquire.** The desire to obtain or collect physical objects, as well as immaterial qualities like status, power, and influence. Companies that promise to make us wealthy, famous, influential, or powerful connect to this drive.

**The Drive to Bond.** The desire to feel valued and loved by forming relationships with others, either platonic or romantic. Companies that promise to make us attractive, well liked, or highly regarded connect to this drive.

**The Drive to Learn.** The desire to satisfy our curiosity. Companies that promise to make us more knowledgeable or competent connect to this drive.

**The Drive to Defend.** The desire to protect ourselves, our loved ones, and our property. Companies that promise to keep us safe, eliminate a problem, or prevent bad things from happening connect to this drive.

There's a fifth core drive that Lawrence and Nohria missed: **The Drive to Feel.** The desire for new sensory stimulus, intense emotional experiences, pleasure, excitement, entertainment, and anticipation. Offers that promise to give us pleasure, thrill us, or give us something to look forward to connect with this drive.

At the core, all successful businesses sell some combination of money, status, power, love, knowledge, protection, pleasure, and excitement. The more clearly you articulate how your product satisfies one or more of these drives, the more attractive your offer will become.

## 3. Buyer psychology cheatsheet — biases by journey stage (Customer Camp)

"Use these tips and market smarter along the whole buyer's journey."

### Trigger event

Every purchase begins with a trigger event — a moment when a buyer moves from being oblivious that they even have a problem to being in the market for a new solution. Trigger events can be biological (being hungry, being cold), situational (getting engaged, getting a new job), emotional (feeling sad, inspired, or jealous), or social (a fight with a spouse, a suggestion from a friend). Discover your buyers' trigger events and you can get in front of them sooner, in less crowded channels, with better messages.

### Passive looking

- **Distinctiveness** — we're more likely to notice stuff that stands out from the rest. *Action: differentiate (or die).*
- **Mere exposure effect** — the more we see something, the more we come to like and trust it. *Action: show up consistently.*
- **Social currency** — we're more likely to share stuff that makes us look good to others. *Action: make sharing gratifying.*
- **Status quo bias** — we're resistant to change and prefer the known to unknown. *Action: show risk of doing nothing.*
- **The Barnum effect** — we're drawn to statements that feel personal (even if they could apply to anyone). *Action: make it feel personal.*

### Active looking

- **Priming** — we're unconsciously influenced by even the smallest details. *Action: prime people to buy.*
- **Framing** — how something is presented very much impacts our perception of it. *Action: frame your offer carefully.*
- **Anchoring** — the first piece of information we see sets our expectations. *Action: use anchors strategically.*
- **Authority bias** — we're more trusting of authority figures (e.g. doctors, celebrities). *Action: showcase your authority.*
- **Foot-in-door technique** — we're more likely to agree to a bigger request after we've already agreed to a smaller one. *Action: start with a small ask.*

### Evaluation

- **Social proof** — we're more trusting of stuff that other people already trust. *Action: let others sell for you.*
- **Scarcity** — we place a higher value on things when they're in limited supply. *Action: reduce availability.*
- **Bandwagon effect** — we're more likely to do something if everyone else is doing it. *Action: showcase popularity.*
- **Loss aversion** — we're more motivated to avoid losses than we are to receive gains. *Action: highlight what's at stake.*
- **Paradox of choice** — when given too many options we become overwhelmed and may delay choosing altogether. *Action: reduce complexity.*

### Purchase

- **Delight** — when we receive unexpected value, we feel intense joy. *Action: deliver surprise value.*
- **IKEA effect** — we place higher value on things that we had a hand in creating. *Action: co-create with buyers.*
- **Peak-end rule** — we judge an experience by its peak moment and how it ends. *Action: create positive peaks/ends.*
- **Reciprocity** — when we're given something, we feel an urge to return the favor. *Action: give generously.*

## 4. Primary citations — the research behind the cheatsheet

The Customer Camp cheatsheet (§3) names biases without sources. These are the original studies, so a reviewer can check a claim instead of trusting a poster. Every DOI below was confirmed against Crossref on 2026-09-27. "Caveat" flags replication problems — cite those biases as directional, never as a promised lift.

**The cheatsheet's actions apply subject to [copy-guardrails.md](copy-guardrails.md).** In particular, "reduce availability" (scarcity) and "highlight what's at stake" (loss aversion) are allowed only when the scarcity is real and the stake is true and specific (G2, G3, G16).
